Before you go

What you are actually going to see

Québec City is where France landed and stayed. Champlain founded it in 1608 at the narrowest point of the St. Lawrence — kébec is an Algonquin word meaning where the river narrows — on a headland that could be defended. Four centuries later it is still the only city north of Mexico with its defensive walls intact, which is why UNESCO listed it in 1985 as an almost complete fortified colonial town.

The thing that makes it unlike anywhere else in North America is that it is built in two storeys. The Upper Town sits on the cliff at Cap Diamant behind the ramparts; the Lower Town is squeezed onto the strip of shore below, where the wharves were. Sixty vertical metres separate them, crossed by a stairway built in 1635, a funicular running at 45 degrees, and a handful of very steep lanes. Nothing about it reads as Canadian, and that is the point.

Around it: an island in the river that has been farmed since the 1600s, a waterfall taller than Niagara at the edge of the suburbs, and behind all of it the Laurentian plateau, cut by a glacial trench 550 metres deep. You are here at the front edge of peak foliage — Tourisme Québec puts the region's mid-point in the last week of September and its peak in the first week of October.

Where to sleep

Six options, one recommendation

You asked for countryside rather than city, ideally near water. The island delivers that better than anywhere else within reach — you are fifteen minutes from Old Québec but sleeping among farms with the river outside the window. Every property below was checked as trading in August 2026.

What is actually open

Adventure — and the calendar that kills most of it

Your three days are Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday at the turn of October, and that is precisely when this coast switches to a weekend-only schedule or shuts for the year. Four of the five adventures within reach are unavailable on your days. One is open, it is cheap, and it is already on your route along the Beaupré coast.

Everything below was checked against the 2026 season; the two figures still unconfirmed are marked as such rather than guessed.

Openthe one that works

Canyon Sainte-Anne — open, and on your road

A 74-metre waterfall in a granite gorge, crossed by three suspension bridges including one of the tallest in Québec. The Air Canyon double zipline runs 90 m above the canyon floor at up to 50 km/h.

It sits on the Beaupré coast, which day 3 already crosses — so this costs you no detour and no extra day. At $15 a head it is also the cheapest thing in this section by a wide margin.

Air Canyon limits: age 5+, minimum height 107 cm; riders between 107 and 120 cm must be accompanied by an adult. Maximum 136 kg solo, 205 kg for a pair. Barred if pregnant, or with back or heart problems, or after recent surgery.

Ice cider is a genuinely Québec-only thing — a protected designation, made from apples concentrated by natural winter cold. It travels well and it is the best souvenir on this trip. If you would rather have mountains than a city on Wednesday

Parc national de la Jacques-Cartier is thirty minutes north — a glacial trench over 550 m deep, $10.30 a head. The famous trail is Les Loups: 5 km and about two hours to the first belvedere, or 11 km and three and a half hours for the full thing, rated difficult with 447 m of climbing. It has a hard cap on hiker numbers and closes when full, so arrive early. The visitor centre runs 09:00–16:30 on a Wednesday. Canoe rental closed on the 28th.

Two gentler nature options: Canyon Sainte-Anne on the Beaupré coast, three suspension bridges with one 60 m above the gorge, and Wendake twenty minutes north, where the Huron-Wendat traditional site sits beside the Kabir Kouba falls — a 28 m drop into a canyon 42 m deep.
