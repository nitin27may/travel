Before the days start

What Norway physically is

The fjords are glacial, not tectonic. Ice a kilometre thick ground down river valleys and cut below sea level; when it left, the sea flooded in. That is why the walls are vertical rather than V-shaped, why the water is over a kilometre deep in places while the mouth is shallow — and why waterfalls hang in mid-air off truncated side valleys. The tributary glaciers simply could not cut as deep as the trunk glacier did.

The Arctic islands are a different thing entirely. Lofoten and Senja are gneiss and granite billions of years old, rising straight out of the sea with no foothills. That is why the peaks read as impossibly steep for their modest height: Segla is 639 m and looks Himalayan because it starts at zero.

The specific famous thing is not “the fjords”. Two of them carry the UNESCO listing — Nærøyfjord and Geirangerfjord — and everything else markets itself in their language. And the thing Norway has that the Alps do not is the light: in the last week of June, north of the Arctic Circle, the sun does not set at all. Not “stays light late”. Does not set.

What you cannot get anywhere else is that combination — Alpine relief standing in open ocean, under a sun that circles the horizon instead of crossing it. The cultural half is the fishing coast: stockfish racks, rorbuer built for cod season, villages on skerries with no land to speak of.

The one decision that changes everything

Which fortnight of summer 2028

Fellesferie — Norway’s three-week national shutdown — is ISO weeks 28–30 every year. In 2028 that computes to 10–30 July, a full week later than 2026’s 6–24 July. That one week of drift creates a window 2026 did not have.

~24 June – 8 July 2028 · Arctic first

Entirely ahead of the Norwegian domestic surge, and entirely inside the midnight sun. Svolvær runs 25 May – 18 July, Reine 27 May – 16 July, Senja to 24 July, Tromsø to 25 July. A window ending 8 July clears all five with margin.

The usual objection to late June is snow on the high ridges — and it does not apply here, because no high inland ridge is in this plan. What is in it opens earlier: the Romsdalseggen bus runs from 12 June, the Nibbevegen up Dalsnibba opens mid-to-late May, and Loen and Nigardsbreen run from spring.

This is why the trip is built Arctic-first — reach Lofoten and Senja while the sun does not set, then come south to the fjords.

Arrives as fellesferie ends on 30 July. Every ridge snow-free, every lift and mountain road running, and Lofoten still has no astronomical darkness — civil dusk 23:56, civil dawn 02:29 on 12 August. What you lose is the midnight sun itself, which is gone from Lofoten by 18 July.

Take this only if someone in the group genuinely cannot sleep in continuous daylight, or if a high inland ridge — Besseggen, Trolltunga, Galdhøpiggen — gets added to the plan later. Either would flip the answer.

If you take it, reverse the whole itinerary: fly in to Bergen, drive the fjords north, fly Ålesund → Evenes, and come home from Tromsø. The single-stop Icelandair leg then lands on the way out instead of the way home.

What to book, and who can actually do it

Adventure

Norway prices adventure per person, not per group. That is the opposite of the Dolomites, where a mountain guide charges a group fee that a party of four splits four ways. Here a second person doubles the bill and a fourth quadruples it — so group size drives the total and never the unit cost. Budget accordingly.

The thing that disqualifies people is weight and age, not fitness. Via Ferrata Loen turns on a 40–120 kg window set by the harness and lanyard system, not by policy, so there is nothing to negotiate on the day. Check that first, before anyone books a flight around it.

Two of these compete for the same slot. Day 12 is the ferrata or the glacier, not both — Nigardsbreen is an hour and twenty from Loen and its walks start between 09:45 and 12:30, which collides with the 09:00–11:00 ferrata departures.

The day-12 choice

Ferrata if the group clears 40 kg and wants exposure with a rope on it. Loen’s route ends at Hoven and you ride the Skylift down, so there is no descent on tired arms.

Glacier if anyone is under 12, or over 120 kg, or would rather walk on ice than climb rock. Nigardsbreen takes children from 8 on the short walk, and the grades differ by time on the ice — 1.5 h, 2.5 h or 4 h — not by how hard the approach is.

Either way, book before you go: both run small groups with fixed start times, and the ice-climbing variant caps at six people.

The two sea days

The Trollfjord RIB on day 2 is a rigid inflatable in open water — cold and exposed even in July, suits provided. It runs into a fjord 100 m wide with walls to 1,000 m, through one of Europe’s densest white-tailed eagle populations.

The Andenes whale safari on day 5 goes out to the continental shelf edge, which is why sperm whales are there reliably rather than seasonally. It is genuinely rough water. If anyone is prone to seasickness this is the day it will show, and it lands immediately before a two-hour ferry crossing.

Day 3 · 22:00

Reinebringen — walk it at night timed

1,978 Sherpa-built stone steps — 2 km round trip on the trail itself, about 5 km if you count the walk from the nearest paid lot. 448 m of gain, 1.5–3.5 hours. Not technical, just relentless. The Fiskebøl–Melbu ferry — worth it only if you time it

It is free — cars, vans, motorcycles and passengers alike, no AutoPASS registration — takes 25 minutes, runs every two hours from 06:20 (last from Melbu 21:30), and takes no reservations. It cuts the corner across Hadselfjorden.

But the popular claim that it saves “at least two hours” does not apply to this leg. That figure compares against a 200 km detour via Sortland and Lødingen; the all-road route from Lofoten to Andenes already runs via Sortland and never touches Lødingen. The real saving here is plausibly 45–60 minutes.

And on a two-hourly service, arriving five minutes after a sailing costs more than the shortcut saves. Check the departure before committing; otherwise just drive.

Day 6

Hesten — and Segla too, if you want it

Climb Hesten, not Segla. The famous photograph of Segla is taken from Hesten; from Segla’s own summit you cannot see the thing that makes it famous. Hesten is graded hard despite the modest numbers and is not for anyone uneasy with exposure.

Both fit in one day. Segla’s summit is 5 km return and 610 m — about 10.3 km for the pair, inside the ceiling. The catch: the trailheads sit 600 m apart in Fjordgård and the two routes cannot be linked; each is its own out-and-back.

Then Tungeneset and Bergsbotn on the National Tourist Route. Senja is the deliberate anti-Lofoten: the same landscape with a fraction of the traffic. The trade is thin accommodation, thin restaurants, and narrow roads full of campervans — so drive the route early or late, which the light makes trivial.

Romsdalseggen the big one

Ten kilometres along a ridge with Trollveggen on one side and Romsdalsfjord on the other. The logistics are already solved, which is why this beats Trolltunga on a trip like this: a dedicated bus leaves platform 5 at Norsk Tindesenter, twenty minutes to the Vengedalen start, and you walk back down into town. Map — Norsk TindesenterThe Romsdalseggen busVisitNorway — route & safety
The descent is the longer half

It climbs 970 m and drops 1,277 m into Åndalsnes. The descent is bigger than the ascent and it is what will be felt the next day — poles are worth carrying. Distance is quoted between 10 and 11 km depending on the source; all of them sit inside the 12 km ceiling.

Exposed ridge, no shelter. The same base gives three fallbacks that need no weather: the gondola, Rampestreken, and Via Ferrata Romsdalsstigen — reputedly the hardest via ferrata in Norway.

Bus runs daily from 12 June to mid-October and tickets go on sale in advance. GAP: 2028 fare and sale-opening date.

The limits — checked, and not negotiable

Recommended from age 12. Minimum weight 40 kg, maximum 120 kg. Both weights are set by the harness and lanyard system rather than by policy, so there is no arguing them on the day. No height limit is published.

Bring a pack with at least 1.5 L of water per person, food, gloves, long trousers, a windproof and a buff. Low hiking shoes with Vibram soles are recommended; Loen Active rents La Sportiva shoes and sells gloves on site.

Day 15 · only if clear

Ulriken by cable car — Vidden is cut weather lottery

The Vidden traverse is out. Ulriken across the plateau to Fløyen is 13–15 km — one source says 18 — and the quoted 4–6 hours becomes 7–8 for most people. That is over the ceiling, on the last morning, with a flight to catch.

Take the cable car to Ulriken, 643 m, and come back down the same way. Same view over city, fjord and islands, no distance, and nothing at risk.

Three levers that actually move it: self-cater breakfast and lunch (book kitchens — this beats any pass or card); buy alcohol at Vinmonopolet, which is closed Sundays and Saturdays only to 16:00; and remember the best hours are free — Ørnesvingen, Flydalsjuvet, Aksla, every hike here, and the whole tourist-route viewpoint network.

Driving in Norway

  • ~3 km/h camera tolerance, and section control is standard in tunnels. NOK 3,350 for 6–10 km/h over; about NOK 10,100 at 20 over.
  • Limits: 50 urban, 30 residential, 80 rural, 90–100 motorway.
  • Tolls are automatic via AutoPASS and billed back through the rental with a NOK 30–80/day handling fee on days you incur any.
  • Ferries bill the same way. The full 50% ferry discount needs a prepaid agreement a visitor in a rental generally cannot get — assume full fare.
  • EVs are the default rental. 10,000+ fast chargers at NOK 4–8/kWh, and EVs pay materially less on ferries. GAP: charger density on Sognefjellet and the Senja route.
