Before the days start

What Iceland physically is

Iceland is the only place on earth where a mid-ocean ridge sits above sea level. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge runs the length of the island and a mantle plume sits underneath it — that combination is the only reason there is land here at all instead of two and a half kilometres of water. The North American and Eurasian plates are pulling apart at roughly 2 cm a year, and at Þingvellir you walk down into the rift they leave behind.

Everything else follows from that one fact. The fissure eruptions. The geothermal fields you can smell before you see. The black sand, which is basalt ground down by ice and sea. And the glaciers sitting directly on top of active volcanoes — which is why Icelandic has a word, jökulhlaup, for the flood that happens when one erupts underneath the other.

Why it is famous, specifically. Not “waterfalls”. The compression. You can stand between two continental plates in the morning, walk on a glacier tongue after lunch, and sleep beside a lava field that was liquid within living memory — without driving more than three hours.

What you cannot get anywhere else. Þingvellir is both the rift valley and the site of the Alþingi, founded in 930 AD — the oldest surviving parliament in the world, held in a crack between two continents. And the pool culture: every town has a heated outdoor pool with hot pots, open late, at roughly a tenth of the price of the tourist lagoons. In a van, that is your daily shower and your evening out.

The one decision that set everything else

Why early September, and not summer

I computed the sun angles for eight points on this route with the NOAA algorithm (sun-times.py and dark-times.py in the trip folder — reproducible, nothing quoted). Iceland is UTC+0 all year, so there is no daylight-saving correction to argue about.

The result is blunter than expected: Iceland gets no astronomical darkness at all before about 3 September. In mid-July it does not even reach the end of civil twilight. An aurora on a summer van trip is not unlikely — it is physically impossible.

That pushes the trip later. Three things push it earlier: campsites shut through September (50 close during the month, 22 more in October, and only ~23 publish an all-year season); the Camping Card expires 15 September; and the weather degrades. The two curves cross in a narrow band, and this window sits in it.

4 – 15 September 2027 · 11 nights

The route is deliberately counter-clockwise, and the reason is the darkness table above. The south coast comes first, when there is barely any dark and it does not matter. By the time the trip reaches Mývatn and the north on nights 7–9 (10–12 September), true darkness has arrived: 2.3 h, then 3.0 h, and it falls around midnight, not in the evening. The aurora chances land in the half of the trip that is furthest from city light.

It also puts the most weather-exposed stretch — the south coast, where the gusts are worst — on the earliest and statistically driest days of the window.

Last camping night is 14 September, one day inside the Camping Card’s validity. That is not a coincidence; it is the constraint that fixed the end date.

First van trip — read this before booking anything

The van rules, and the limits that decide who can do what

Three constraints do more to shape this trip than any scenery does. Two are legal and one is physical, and none of them are negotiable.

You sleep at a campsite or you break the law

Since the 2015 tightening of the Nature Conservation Act, overnighting in a campervan or motorhome is legal only at a marked campsite. The Icelandic right to roam does not extend to vehicles. Pulling over somewhere remote for the night is not a grey area; enforcement is active, fines are real, and illegal parking can compromise the rental’s insurance.

Read it as a feature. There are 170+ registered campsites and one is almost always within reach, so the worst part of first-time van travel — the 18:00 scramble for somewhere legal — simply does not happen here.

But it cancels the usual van superpower. You cannot wake up at a trailhead. Days get planned around campsite locations, and campsites are in towns.

F-roads are off the table in this vehicle

2WD vehicles are not permitted on F-roads. Taking one up there voids the entire insurance policy and risks a fine under Icelandic traffic law.

Behind that sits a harder limit: no Icelandic insurer offers river-crossing cover at any price. Not as an add-on, not at all. F-roads have unbridged rivers. Flood the van and you have bought it — they are worth 8–12 million ISK.

So Landmannalaugar, Þórsmörk, Askja and Kerlingarfjöll are out of this trip. Accepted deliberately: remote unsignposted country with no phone signal is not where you learn what a van does. If the interior matters later, it needs an F-road-legal 4x4 camper, or a highland bus with the van left in town.

Reynisfjara — the one that is actually dangerous

Sneaker waves here run far higher up the beach than the previous ten waves suggest, and people have died. It also changed this year: a landslide and severe coastal erosion in February 2026 narrowed the shoreline and largely destroyed one of the viewing platforms. Access is now managed by safety zone and can be restricted depending on surf, tide and wind.

Since August 2025, entering Hálsanefshellir cave is prohibited whenever the red warning light is flashing. Stay high on the beach. Never turn your back on the water. This is the single stop on the trip where the rule matters more than the photo.

What September costs you, honestly

The puffins are gone. They leave Iceland from about mid-August, and from 15 August operators stop selling combined whale-and-puffin trips altogether. Dyrhólaey will have empty cliffs.

There is no window that has both puffins and aurora — one needs summer, the other needs darkness, and in Iceland those never overlap. This plan chooses the aurora.

Whale watching still works: humpback and minke are the September species at Húsavík. Blue whales are early-season only.

The volcano question, answered honestly

No eruption is under way. The Sundhnúkur sequence that began in December 2023 has slowed — the ninth eruption ran 16 July – 5 August 2025. But uplift continues at Svartsengi and the magma volume is the highest since the sequence started, so another eruption or dike intrusion is possible.

Grindávík and the Blue Lagoon are open; the town has crevasses, so stay on paths. Treat this whole peninsula as a bonus, never a dependency — check safetravel.is the morning of, and if it is shut, drive straight to Selfoss and lose nothing that matters.

Þingvellir — walk down into the rift the point

Almannagjá is the eastern edge of the North American plate, and the path runs along the bottom of it. The valley floor between here and the Eurasian wall has dropped as the two pulled apart.

At the foot of it sits the Alþingi site, founded 930 AD — the oldest surviving parliament in the world, held annually in a crack between two continents. That combination does not exist anywhere else, and it is why this is the first real stop.

The specific rules, as of now

Since August 2025 it is prohibited to enter Hálsanefshellir cave whenever the red warning light is flashing. Depending on surf, tide and wind, other areas near the basalt columns can be restricted or closed outright.

Practical version: stay on the dry sand well above the highest wet line, keep the sea in front of you at all times, and do not walk out onto the black sand to take a picture facing inland. That is the posture people are in when they get taken.

Check the wind forecast for Vík on vedur.is before you commit to this stop. In a strong onshore southerly, look from Dyrhólaey instead and skip the beach.

Fjaðrárgljúfur is a 100 m serpentine canyon lined with moss, with a rim path along the top. No entrance fee; lower car park 1,000 ISK, upper car park free.

Dettifoss — take the west road the loudest

The most powerful waterfall in Europe by volume — roughly 193 m wide and 44 m high, carrying grey glacial meltwater off Vatnajökull. You feel it through the ground before you see it.

Hverir — the one that smells unlike anywhere

A geothermal field at the foot of Námafjöll: ochre and grey mud pots boiling at the surface, steam vents roaring out of the ground under pressure, and no vegetation at all because the soil is too acidic to support any.

Dimmuborgir, Hverfjall and Grjótagjá

Dimmuborgir is a labyrinth of lava pillars formed when a lava lake drained away and left the hollow columns standing. Marked loops from 10 minutes to an hour.

Hverfjall is a 2,500-year-old tephra crater you can walk the rim of — the rim is about 3 km around, 30–45 minutes up from the car park, roughly an hour to circle, 1.5–2 hours total. It is exposed; skip it in strong wind.

Grjótagjá is a hot spring in a lava cave. Look, do not swim — it has run too hot since the Kröflu fires and it is private land.

Húsavík — whales book ahead

Skálfandi bay is the most reliable whale water in Iceland. September species are humpback and minke; blue whales are early-season only, and the puffins have gone — operators stop selling combined whale-and-puffin trips from 15 August.

Take the morning sailing: the bay is calmest early, and it leaves the afternoon for Goðafoss and Akureyri.

GeoSea afterwards — clifftop geothermal seawater baths looking out over the same bay you have just been on.

Goðafoss

Right on Route 1, thirty seconds from the car park, and still one of the best waterfalls on the island — a 12 m horseshoe in the Skjálfandafljót.

The name means “waterfall of the gods”: when Iceland converted to Christianity around the year 1000, the lawspeaker Þorgeir is said to have thrown his pagan idols in.

Day 13 · 08:00

The longest drive of the trip — break it on purpose

~390 km, about five hours of driving. This is the day the trip could go wrong: it is long, it is mostly transit, and it comes after eight days of early starts.

Leave early, and stop three times whether or not you feel like it. Hvítserkur, a 15 m basalt sea stack that looks like an animal drinking; the Hvammstangi seal colony on the way past; then Hraunfossar, where a waterfall seeps out of the face of a lava field along nearly a kilometre of cliff, with Deildartunguhver — Europe’s highest-flow hot spring — a few minutes further on.

If the day is too much

Split it. Stop at Borgarnes instead of pushing to Grundarfjörður, then do Snæfellsnes as a loop out and back on Day 14 and drive to Reykjavík that evening. It costs you the Kirkjufell sunrise and nothing else. Borgarnes campsite is also one of the year-round sites, so it is the safest fallback on the whole route.

Day 14 · 08:00

Kirkjufell, and Snæfellsnes properly best value

Snæfellsnes is called “Iceland in miniature” and it earns it — glacier, volcano, lava field, black beach, bird cliffs and fishing villages inside 90 km.

Kirkjufell with Kirkjufellsfoss in front of it is the most photographed mountain in the country; it is a two-minute walk from a car park across the road from the campsite. Go at first light, before the Reykjavík day-tour buses arrive around 11:00.

Djúpalónssandur and Snæfellsjökull

A black pebble beach with the rusted remains of the trawler Epine GY7, wrecked in 1948, still lying scattered across it — left deliberately as a memorial, so do not move anything. Four lifting stones sit at the top of the path; fishermen were graded by which one they could get onto a ledge, and the lightest is 23 kg.

Above it, Snæfellsjökull — the glacier-capped volcano Jules Verne used as the way in to the centre of the earth.

A 2.5 km coastal path between two villages along columnar basalt cliffs, sea arches and a fulmar colony — flat, easy, and the best short walk on the trip. Walk it one way and back, or leave the van at one end.

Reykjavík, Laugardalur — night 11

Year-round campsite next to the city’s biggest pool, a short hop from the van depot in the morning. Sleeping here rather than out on the peninsula is deliberate — it removes a two-and-a-half-hour drive from the departure day.

Reykjavík, Sky Lagoon, and the drive to KEF

Hallgrímskirkja’s tower for the coloured-roof view — and note the facade, which is basalt columns, the same geometry you walked up to at Svartifoss on day 7. Then Harpa and the old harbour.
